## What we learned

- No homeowner or architect has used Nirman. The founder confirmed: “I never tested it in real life by asking someone.”
- One person suggested an improvement, and an architect has said they will try the product when it is ready. These are feedback and access signals, not evidence of usage or demand.
- The current problem statement is a hypothesis: homeowners may be unhappy when a built home differs from what they imagined, while architects may struggle to understand clients' instructions. Neither group has described this pain directly to the founder yet.
- The founder has spent about 15 days building Nirman for a college viva and presentation on 3 December. Their immediate goals are stability, understanding the code, and a reliable demo; the startup direction is longer term.
- Static inspection of the clean `main` branch found a browser prototype in `lab.html`, `draw.js`, `house.js`, `threeD.js`, and `nirman_house_model.json`. Source code includes an SVG plan editor, room and door/window editing paths, local-storage save/reset logic, and a call from the 2D renderer to the 3D update function. This was a source read only; the app was not run, so none of those flows are runtime-verified.

## The decision

Keep the startup hypothesis alive, but do not call the problem validated or add major startup features before the viva. Rehearse the existing demo before 3 December, then run problem discovery after the presentation. The first discovery should compare homeowners who are planning a house with architects, since the founder currently describes both as possible users with different needs.

## The one action

From 4–17 December, complete 20 problem conversations: 10 with homeowners planning or building a home and 10 with architects. Ask each person about a recent layout or client-communication problem, what they did to handle it, and what it cost in time, money, or rework. Let them describe their experience before showing Nirman.

## KILL/CONTINUE

By 17 December, if those 20 conversations do not produce repeated recent examples of a costly planning or communication problem within one group, change or narrow the problem before adding startup features.

## First week

1. Before 3 December, rehearse the current demo with three classmates or teachers and note where they get confused. Treat this as presentation practice, not customer validation.
2. Keep the four pre-viva days focused on stability, code understanding, and the demo; record unfinished features instead of expanding scope.
3. After the presentation, schedule the 20 conversations across the two groups.
4. Ask about the most recent real example and current workaround before describing Nirman's proposed solution.
5. By 17 December, compare the evidence by role and decide whether to continue, narrow, or change the problem statement.

## Open questions

- Which group has the stronger, more frequent problem: homeowners planning a home or architects translating client requests?
- Is the primary value helping homeowners explore a design, helping architects clarify requirements, or connecting both in one workflow?
- Which current 2D/3D flows work reliably enough to demonstrate? This needs a runtime check before any feature is described as working.

## What I noticed about how you think

- You said, “we are just assuming that it's going to be a problem.” You separated your belief about the problem from evidence that a user has it.
- You described both the homeowner's wish to shape a once-in-a-lifetime home and the architect's difficulty understanding a client. That points to two possible users whose needs should be tested separately.
- You chose to rehearse the demo before the viva and reserve interviews for afterward, keeping your immediate deadline and startup goal in view.
